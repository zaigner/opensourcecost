What a free package costs

Companies download Python packages billions of times a month at no charge. Someone still pays to keep each one working and secure. This brief sets out a method for putting a unit cost on a single package, then applies it to urllib3, the HTTP library underneath requests, pip, boto3 and most cloud SDKs.

The question

Why "free" is the wrong price

An open source license sets the purchase price to zero. It does not set the cost to zero. Review, security response, releases, compatibility with new Python versions and user support all take skilled hours, and those hours are mostly unpaid. In Tidelift's 2024 survey of over 400 maintainers, 60% described themselves as unpaid hobbyists. Black Duck's audits found open source in 97% of commercial codebases in 2025, with an average of 911 components per application.

The cost does not disappear when no one pays it. It turns into slower fixes, a bus factor of one, burnout, and eventually an incident. The xz-utils backdoor of 2024 got in through exactly that gap: a lone, overworked maintainer was pressured into handing over control. CISA's response called on companies that profit from open source to fund it. When a widely used package fails, every downstream user pays the bill at once. During Log4Shell, a single US federal department reported spending 33,000 hours on its response.

The existing headline estimates answer a different question. Harvard Business School's 2024 study valued widely used open source at $4.15 billion to recreate and $8.8 trillion in demand-side value. Those are asset values for the whole ecosystem. They don't tell a company what one package costs per year, or what its fair share of that cost is. This brief builds that number.

The framework

Total Cost of Stewardship

Four measures. Each answers a different question, and each comes from public data.

RV

Replacement value

What it would cost to rebuild the code and its test suite from scratch. COCOMO II on measured source lines.

Asset value. A ceiling, never a price.
ASC

Annual sustaining cost

What it costs each year to keep the package maintained, secure and released to a responsible standard.

The unit cost. This is what gets priced.
DRC

Downstream remediation cost

What dependents spend each year responding to the package's security advisories.

The cost of underfunding, paid by users.
FS

Fair share

Each commercial beneficiary's slice of the sustaining cost, under an allocation key the company can audit.

The price on the invoice.

Measuring the sustaining cost three independent ways

No single method can tell you what maintenance should cost, so the model takes three that rest on different evidence and uses the median as the point estimate. Where the three agree, the number is hard to dismiss.

  • Activity-based. Count the actual workload over the last 12 months (pull requests, issues, releases, advisories, maintainer commits) and multiply each item by a standard time. This measures the work currently being done, most of it unpaid.
  • COCOMO maintenance. The standard software-estimation model sets annual maintenance effort to annual change traffic (the share of the codebase that changes in a year, measured from git history) times the original development effort.
  • Responsible staffing. A normative floor. A critical package needs at least two maintainers, so no one person is a single point of failure, plus security response capacity and a reserve for audits and infrastructure. Packages are tiered by downloads and dependents.

Every hour is priced at the fully loaded cost of a US software developer: the BLS median wage of $133,080 (May 2024) divided by 0.701, the wage share of total compensation in BLS employer cost data (December 2025). That gives $189,843 a year, $104.08 an hour, or $15,820 per COCOMO person-month of 152 hours.

Results

The unit cost

The three methods land between $170,042 and $369,587 a year, about 0.9 to 2 full-time engineers. The point estimate is $257,304. The range is a finding in its own right. The activity method measures what volunteers already do. The COCOMO method measures what a codebase changing at this rate would normally need. The gap between them is roughly the work that would get done with adequate staffing.

The benchmarks agree. Germany's Sovereign Tech Agency has awarded similar critical projects €195,000 (curl), €200,000 (OpenSSH) and €223,000 (PHP, 2025–26) for scoped maintenance programs.

Rebuilding urllib3 from scratch would take about 122 person-months, a replacement value of $1,926,282. That figure is the make-versus-buy ceiling. It is not the price. As critics of the HBS study have pointed out, rebuild cost overstates what users would actually pay.

Two numbers frame the case. Spread across the 2,000 largest companies, keeping urllib3 healthy costs each about $102.92 a year. A company that lost upstream maintenance and had to run its own fork would carry the full $257,304 alone. Meanwhile, the open source packages that depend directly on urllib3 spend an estimated $7.5 million a year reacting to its advisories, 29 times what prevention would cost. That remediation figure is a floor. It counts only public PyPI packages, at one hour each, and leaves out every private corporate codebase.

Using the model

How to defend the number

  • Lead with ASC, not replacement value. A trillion-dollar asset figure invites the reply that nobody would ever pay it. An annual cost of about $257,000, checked three ways and in line with public grant sizes, is much harder to wave away.
  • Show the leverage. The downstream remediation cost runs about 29 times the sustaining cost, using only public dependents. Funding maintenance upstream is cheaper than paying for incidents downstream.
  • Make the ask small and specific. The fair share for one critical package is around $100 a year per large company. The finding is that open source is cheap to sustain. The problem is coordination, because each company assumes another will pay.
  • Price the tree, not the package. Companies carry hundreds of dependencies. The invoice above turns those counts into one annual figure a budget owner can approve.

Limitations

  • Standard times per pull request, issue and advisory are assumptions. They are exposed as controls so readers can apply their own.
  • COCOMO II was calibrated on commercial projects. Volunteer projects with heavy test suites may sit outside its range.
  • Download counts include CI runs and mirrors, so per-download prices understate cost per real user.
  • deps.dev counts public PyPI dependents only. Private corporate use is invisible, so the remediation cost is a floor.
  • Open Collective is the only funding channel visible from outside. Maintainers may also get employer time or grants that don't show up there, so the true coverage may be higher than 6%. Of the $69,331 raised on Open Collective since 2016, about $15,900 came from one long-time maintainer.
  • The staffing tiers, commercial share α, size weights and paying pool are policy choices. They are not empirical measurements, and the brief presents them that way.
